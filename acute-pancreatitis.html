Acute Pancreatitis

What is acute pancreatitis?

Acute pancreatitis results from the leakage of pancreatic enzymes into the pancreatic tissue, leading to autodigestion.

The cell damage makes the immune system respond, which causes inflammation of the whole body.

Causes

Common causes and triggers of acute pancreatitis.

Main causes
  • Alcohol
  • Gallstones
  • Idiopathic causes (cause not known)
  • Trauma
  • Infections (mumps, mycoplasma, echovirus)
  • Vasculitis (SLE, necrotizing angiitis)
  • Drugs
  • Autoimmune pancreatitis
  • Genetic mutations

Symptoms

What the patient feels.

General
  • Fever, chills, or both
  • Blood pressure becomes extremely low
Heart
  • Rapid heartbeat
Stomach
  • Sudden abdominal pain
  • Nausea
  • Abdomen tender to the touch
  • Difficult to lie on a hard surface
  • Pain often begins or worsens after eating
Other signs and symptoms
  • Acute onset fever
  • Nausea and vomiting
  • Pain in the epigastric area and left upper quadrant
  • May have ileus and signs of shock

Abdominal examination

What the doctor finds.

Findings
  • Guarding and tenderness in the epigastric area, which may radiate.
  • Possible blue or purple discoloration of the skin.
Cullen’s sign

Discoloration around the umbilicus.

Grey Turner’s sign

Discoloration on the flanks.

Ileus

Gas is spread uniformly through the bowel on imaging.

Diet and regimen

Advice for eating.

Eat
  • High protein
  • Nutrient-dense diets
  • Fruits, vegetables and whole grains
  • Low fat dairy
Avoid
  • Alcohol
  • Greasy or fried foods

Treatment

General treatment of acute pancreatitis.

Treatment
  • Pain medicine
  • Fluids given through a vein (IV)
  • Stopping food or fluid by mouth
  • Draining fluid that has collected in or around the pancreas
  • Removing gallstones
  • Relieving blockages of the pancreatic duct
Medicines listed on the chart
  • Aconite
  • Aloe
  • Alumina
  • Apis
  • Argentum nitricum
  • Arnica
  • Belladonna

Severe acute pancreatitis

Management starts with a clinical evaluation. After that, it depends on whether the pancreatic tissue has died (necrosis) and whether the dead tissue is infected.

Clinical evaluation
  • Cardiac monitoring
  • Pain control
  • Vascular filling
  • Organ failure support
  • Enteral feeding
  • Antibiotic prophylaxis?
  • Contrast-enhanced CT scan
ERCP: is the cause biliary?
  • Mild acute pancreatitis: no ERCP.
  • Severe acute pancreatitis: no ERCP.
  • Associated cholangitis: ERCP 24 hours later.
  • Obstructive jaundice: ERCP.
Acute necrotizing pancreatitis

Part of the pancreas has died. If the dead tissue is not infected (non-infected necrosis), the treatment is conservative: support for organ failure, with neither drainage nor necrosectomy.

Infected necrosis

The infection is suspected or confirmed. If clinically possible, treatment is delayed until more than 4 weeks after the onset of symptoms, even in case of organ failure. Then the step-up approach is used.

Step-up approach
  • 1) Drainage: image-guided percutaneous or trans-gastric endoscopic.
  • 2) If there is no clinical or biological improvement: minimally-invasive necrosectomy, video-assisted retroperitoneal or endoscopic.
  • 3) If there is still no improvement: necrosectomy via laparotomy.
Exceptions
  • Acute haemorrhage uncontrolled by the endovascular approach
  • Gastro-intestinal ischaemia
  • Abdominal compartment syndrome
  • Gastro-intestinal or biliary obstruction persisting more than several weeks
