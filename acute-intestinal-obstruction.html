Acute Intestinal Obstruction

What is intestinal obstruction?

Intestinal obstruction is a blockage that stops food, fluid and gas from passing normally through the bowel.

Acute obstruction is a surgical emergency that needs urgent treatment.

Classification explained

1. Types of obstruction

Bowel obstruction is either mechanical or non-mechanical.

Mechanical (dynamic)

A physical blockage of the bowel. It is divided into small bowel obstruction (80%) and large bowel obstruction (20%).

Non-mechanical (adynamic)

The bowel stops moving, without a physical blockage. It can involve the whole intestine (pan-intestinal) or only the colon.

Small bowel obstruction (80%)
  • Adhesions
  • Hernia
  • Malignancy
  • Intussusception
  • Others
Large bowel obstruction (20%)
  • Colorectal cancer (60%)
  • Volvulus (5%)
  • Diverticular stricture (20%)
Pan-intestinal

Paralytic ileus.

Colonic

Acute colonic pseudo-obstruction.

2. Causes by site

Where is the cause?

Intraluminal (in the lumen)
  • Faecal impaction
  • Foreign body
  • Intussusception
Intramural (in the wall)
  • Tumour
  • Inflammatory stricture
  • Diverticular disease
Extraluminal (outside the wall)
  • Adhesions
  • Hernia
  • Volvulus

3. Other ways to classify

Obstruction can also be grouped by onset, site and nature.

By onset
  • Acute
  • Chronic
By site
  • Small bowel (high)
  • Large bowel (low)
By nature
  • Simple: the blood supply is not compromised
  • Strangulated: the blood supply is compromised

What happens inside the bowel

  1. The obstruction occurs.
  2. The bowel before the block (proximal) widens and fills with gas and fluid. The bowel after it (distal) collapses.
  3. Fluid is lost into the third space. The bowel contracts harder, causing colicky pain.
  4. Pressure inside the bowel rises.
  5. Veins are compressed, so drainage falls and the wall swells (oedema). Arteries are compressed, causing ischaemia, then necrosis and bacterial overgrowth.
  6. Necrosis of the bowel wall leads to perforation and leakage.
  7. This causes peritonitis and shock, with pain, guarding and fever.

Assessment and investigations

Assessment

Not all the features need to be present.

Four cardinal features
  • Colicky abdominal pain: usually the first symptom.
  • Distension: more marked in large bowel obstruction.
  • Absolute constipation (no faeces and no flatus): early in low obstruction, later in high obstruction.
  • Vomiting: early in high obstruction, later in low obstruction.
Features suggesting strangulation
  • Colicky pain becomes continuous.
  • Pyrexia (fever).
  • Bowel sounds are absent.
  • Tenderness and rigidity.
Examination
  • Assess fluid status.
  • Look for the cause, such as an external hernia or surgical scars.
  • Feel for focal tenderness and guarding, which develop with ischaemia.
  • Listen for characteristic tinkling bowel sounds. Absence suggests peritonitis.

Investigations

Imaging first, then blood tests.

Imaging
  • Erect chest X-ray (CXR): may show pneumoperitoneum.
  • Abdominal X-ray (AXR): shows loops of distended bowel and air-fluid levels.
  • Small bowel: ladder pattern with striations.
  • Large bowel: peripheral location with haustrations.
  • CT with contrast: increasingly the initial imaging of choice.
  • Water-soluble contrast study.
Blood tests
  • FBC, CRP, U&Es, LFTs
  • Group and save
  • VBG (venous blood gas)

Treatment

Treatment: initial management

Acute obstruction is a surgical emergency. Resuscitate and stabilise the patient before surgery.

SUCK

Insert a nasogastric (NG) tube to decompress the bowel.

DRIP

Start IV fluids to replace losses from third spacing and vomiting, and correct electrolyte imbalances.

Other steps
  • Insert a urinary catheter and monitor fluid balance.
  • Nil by mouth.
  • Analgesia.
  • Antibiotics if strangulation is likely.

Treatment: surgery

Urgent laparotomy is needed in these situations.

When surgery is needed
  • Suspected ischaemia or closed loop obstruction.
  • The cause needs surgical treatment.
During surgery
  • Rectify the underlying cause.
  • Inspect the affected bowel to see if it is still viable.
Signs of non-viable bowel
  • Loss of peristalsis
  • Loss of normal sheen
  • Greenish or black colour
  • Loss of arterial pulsation
Removing non-viable bowel
  • Small bowel: primary anastomosis is possible because of its excellent blood supply.
  • Large bowel: often needs a colostomy.

Pathway explained

Follow the chart from top to bottom.

Clinically stable?

An unstable patient goes straight to exploratory laparotomy. A stable patient has imaging first.

Radiography or CT

Imaging shows whether there is vascular compromise or perforation, and whether the obstruction is complete or partial.

Vascular compromise or perforation?

If yes, the patient needs exploratory laparotomy. If no, treatment starts like a complete obstruction, without surgery.

No oral intake, NG tube, IV fluids

Nothing by mouth, a nasogastric tube to decompress the bowel, and intravenous fluids. This is used for complete and partial obstruction.

Resolution within 24 to 48 hours?

Complete obstruction: if it has not resolved, the patient needs laparotomy. Partial obstruction: if it has not resolved, a contrast study is done (upper gastrointestinal, small bowel follow-through or enteroclysis). If that shows no resolution, the patient needs laparotomy. In every case, resolution means the diet can be advanced.

Exploratory laparotomy

Surgery to open the abdomen, find the cause and treat it.

Advance diet

Once the obstruction has resolved, the diet is increased gradually.

Key takeaways

  • Obstruction is an important surgical condition that must not be missed.
  • It can lead to significant illness and death.
  • Resuscitate urgently.
  • Early diagnosis and intervention before significant strangulation occurs can improve outcomes.
